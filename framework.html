The Framework

Competence doesn't travel.
Interpretation does.

Everything published at Sawubona Trail rests on one idea: that being capable and being recognised as capable are two different things — and that the gap between them is where most careers are quietly decided.

The Core Idea

We were taught the wrong equation.

Most career advice assumes a straight line. Do good work, get recognised, move up. Merit in, opportunity out.

That line has a step missing — and the missing step is where everything happens.

What we're told
Competence → Opportunity
What actually happens
Competence → Interpretation → Trust → Opportunity

Nobody ever sees your competence directly. They see a reading of it.

Your capability has to pass through someone else's reading of it before it becomes anything. A manager decides whether your pause meant thoughtfulness or uncertainty. A panel decides whether your directness read as clarity or aggression. A recruiter decides whether ten years somewhere unfamiliar counts as experience or as a gap.

Interpretation is not a bias problem sitting on top of a fair system. It is the system. And every additional layer — gender, migration, an accent, motherhood, a qualification nobody recognises — adds another place where capability can be read wrong.

Who does the interpreting
Managers Recruiters Sponsors Organisational culture Social expectation Cultural norms AI systems

One of these is new. It is also the only one you cannot talk to.

Why This Matters Now

The machine didn't invent the problem.
It learned it.

For most of history, interpretation happened in a room. A person misread you, and a person could be argued with — over coffee, over time, by someone who vouched for you.

Hiring tools, promotion models, visa systems and performance algorithms now sit in that same seat. They don't assess capability directly. They can't. They assess the signals that have historically stood in for it: a familiar university, a recognisable employer, a job title in the expected shape, assertive phrasing, speech without pauses.

The system isn't hearing an accent. It's hearing familiarity. And familiarity is often mistaken for competence.

So the question quietly changes. Not are you capable — but do you sound like the people we hired before?

This is interpreted competence running at scale. The same misreading, automated, applied to thousands of people at once, with the reasoning sealed and nobody to appeal to.

It is not hypothetical. In 2015 the UK Home Office began running visa applications through a "streaming tool" that sorted people into red, amber and green lanes, with nationality among the inputs. It was dropped in August 2020, days before a court would have made it explain itself. Immigrants were among the first people in Britain sorted by an algorithm at a border. Now we're sorted by one at a hiring portal.

The Lenses

Where interpretation breaks down.

The theory holds across a lot of situations, which makes it easy to state and hard to use. These are the places it bites hardest — each one a named pattern, each one with an essay behind it.

Lens 01

The Legibility Gap

The distance between what you have done and what a system can read. A career built across three countries is not less valuable than one built in a single city — it's less legible. Unfamiliar employers, an unrecognised university, a title that doesn't map. The information is all there. Nothing in the reader is equipped to decode it.

This is the lens that matters most to automated systems, because a model cannot ask a follow-up question. It scores what it recognises.

Lens 02

The Hyper-Competence Trap

When you can't rely on being read correctly, you make the work undeniable instead. You over-deliver. It is a rational strategy and it works — right up until the level where nobody is checking the work anymore.

Volume of output signals reliability, not judgement. Senior roles are bought with judgement. Hard work stops being a ladder and becomes a ceiling, and the people most likely to hit it are the ones who most needed it to work.

Lens 03

The Immigrant Readiness Gap

There is a difference between you don't have the capability yet and we don't quite see you succeeding at that level. The first is a capability gap and it can be closed with work. The second is a readiness gap, and no amount of work touches it, because it was never about the work.

At senior level, promotion stops being a performance decision and becomes a belief decision — made by people who have never worked with you directly, in rooms you are not in.

What It's For

Naming it is not the same as solving it.

This framework will not make a biased system fair. It isn't meant to. What it does is move the question off the person and onto the mechanism — which is the only place a fix can actually happen.

For the person being misread, it replaces what is wrong with me with what is this system actually measuring, and which part of it can I speak to. That's not motivation. It's information, and it's usable on a Monday morning.

For the people building these systems — and I am one of them — it is a more uncomfortable question. If a model is rewarding familiarity and calling it merit, that is not an edge case to be patched later. It is the thing the model is doing.
